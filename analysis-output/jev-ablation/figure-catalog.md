# Figure Catalog

All figures are in `figures/` as `.pdf` and `.png`. Figures 1 to 6 are generated by
`scripts/analyze_ablation.py`, Figures 7 to 10 by `scripts/analyze_pooled.py`, Figures 11 and 12
by `scripts/analyze_clusters.py`, and Figures 13 to 16 by `scripts/make_followup_figures.py`, all
from the stored runs in `outputs/`. Figure 17 is generated by
`scripts/analyze_operator_experiment.py` and Figure 18 by `scripts/operator_bandit_replay.py`, both
from the controlled operator experiment in `outputs/operator_experiment/`. Figure numbers here
follow the file names, not the figure numbers in main.tex. The source of truth for how each figure is interpreted is
`paper/jev-ablation/main.tex`.

**Revised 2026-09-28.** The Figure 4 and Figure 9 entries previously read Jev's realized operator
picks as Jev's preference. They are not: NAAMSE's shared per-task seed makes the action draw, the
operator draw, and the EXPLORE corpus draw start from the same random number, so MUTATE (the last
action) is taken only when that number is high, and the operator is then drawn with the same high
number from a list sorted by name. Realized picks therefore land on late-alphabet operators. Jev's
intended preferences are in Figure 13. The seeding is the configured behavior of the system under
test, not a bug; we measure its effect rather than change it.

## Figure 1: `figure-01-main-comparison`
- **Purpose:** the primary outcome comparison across all 8 arms.
- **Plotted:** per-run mean score, max score, and number of prompts with score ≥ 80. Dots are
  seeds; ◆ is the mean with a 95% seed-bootstrap CI.
- **Caption must state:** n = 5 runs per arm; Fit=Jev is scored by Jev (grey, not comparable);
  coverage arms are in orange; success means ≥ 80.
- **Key observation:** every CI overlaps. Max score and success counts are driven by one or
  two outlier seeds per arm (Baseline s2, Act=Jev s4, Fit=Jev s2, Cov+Mut=Jev s5). Most runs
  top out at ≈54.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Exists to test RQ1/RQ3 on outcomes. (2) Notice the
  overlap and the outlier-driven means. (3) No arm differences can be claimed. Fit=Jev is placed
  on the common Meta scale by the refusal-gated referee: +3.3 [−3.8, +11.1] versus the baseline
  (`referee_arm_comparison.csv`), about half of its own-judge gap (+6.0).
- **Caveats:** a bootstrap over 5 points gives unstable, too-narrow CIs; zero-inflated
  counts.

## Figure 2: `figure-02-action-mix`
- **Purpose:** shows how each selector actually spent the budget.
- **Plotted:** mean share of EXPLORE / SIMILAR / MUTATE prompts per arm.
- **Key observation:** Act=Jev moves budget from EXPLORE to SIMILAR/MUTATE. Coverage arms are
  about 70% EXPLORE whatever the selector.
- **Interpretation checklist:** confirms the selector switches worked, and shows that the
  coverage objective reduces to exploration.
- **Caveats:** shares are averaged over runs, not weighted by success.

## Figure 3: `figure-03-jev-action-policy`
- **Purpose:** mechanism for RQ1. What policy did Jev implement?
- **Plotted:** mean Jev action probabilities by parent-score bucket (bars), with the static
  threshold weights overlaid (black ticks); n per bucket on the x-axis.
- **Key observation:** Jev's policy changes steadily with score: P(mutate) goes 0.14 → 0.80
  and P(explore) goes 0.37 → 0.04. It favors SIMILAR where the static policy favors EXPLORE
  (scores below 50). Under the coverage objective all parents are below 30, so only one bucket
  is populated.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Shows Jev's decisions are interpretable. (2) Notice how
  the rule differs from the static one at low scores. (3) Supports reporting "what Jev
  chooses" even without an outcome effect.
- **Caveats:** the ≥80 bucket has n = 12 and 50–80 has n = 36. The empty buckets in the
  coverage panel still show static ticks for reference.

## Figure 4: `figure-04-operator-choice`
- **Purpose:** RQ3. How often each operator was actually applied, and how those prompts scored.
- **Plotted:** counts of MUTATE prompts per operator under the uniform selector and under Jev
  (left; pooled over score and coverage arms), and the mean judge score of those prompts (right;
  dashed line at 80). These are *realized* picks, after NAAMSE's shared per-task seed.
- **Key observation:** Jev's 35 realized picks concentrate on two operators,
  `semantic_steganography` (19) and `synonym` (14). This concentration is **not Jev's
  preference**: Jev's own logged probabilities put only 0.03 on `synonym` and spread most of the
  mass over `adversarial_prefix` (0.26), `persona_roleplay` (0.19), and `semantic_steganography`
  (0.19) (Figure 13, `seed_coupling_operators.csv`). The shared seed turns a non-uniform selector
  into picks from the end of the alphabet. No MUTATE step under either selector produced a
  success.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Exists to show which operators reached the target. (2) Notice
  that the red bars sit on late-alphabet operators, which is the seed coupling, not a mode
  collapse of Jev. (3) Read it together with Figure 13; any claim about what Jev prefers must come
  from Figure 13 or Figure 14, not from this figure.
- **Caveats:** few prompts per operator. Realized counts describe NAAMSE as configured,
  including its per-task seeding. The uniform selector is affected only mildly by the coupling.

## Figure 5: `figure-05-best-so-far`
- **Purpose:** search dynamics over iterations.
- **Plotted:** best score so far per iteration, averaged over seeds; coverage arms dashed.
- **Key observation:** every arm plateaus by iteration 2–3 and none improves after
  iteration 3. The exception is Cov+Mut=Jev, where one seed reaches 100 at iteration 5.
  Curves above ≈54 come from single seeds. Act=Jev starts high at iteration 0 because two
  initial EXPLORE prompts happened to score 80 (seeds 2 and 4). That comes before any Jev
  decision matters, so it isn't a selector effect.
- **Caveats:** mean-of-max curves are sensitive to one outlier seed.

## Figure 6: `figure-06-score-distribution`
- **Purpose:** explains why mean score is a weak metric.
- **Plotted:** histogram of scores for all Meta-judged prompts.
- **Key observation:** the score is discrete: refusal ≈20, partial ≈37, benign compliance
  ≈54, and harm ≥ 80 is rare. Means mostly track refusal rate.
- **Interpretation checklist:** argues for success-based and coverage-based metrics, and for
  larger n, over mean score.

## Figure 7: `figure-07-action-outcomes`
- **Purpose:** RQ1 mechanism. Which action actually works at each parent score?
- **Plotted:** child score (mean, run-bootstrap CI), refusal rate, and P(child > parent) by
  parent bucket × action. n is shown above the x-axis. ■ marks the static rule's most likely
  action and ★ marks Jev's.
- **Key observation:** SIMILAR is best below 30, where Jev is right and static is wrong.
  SIMILAR is worst at 30–50, where Jev is wrong (n = 25). SIMILAR and MUTATE are both good
  at 50–80.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Replaces the underpowered arm contrast with pooled
  evidence. (2) Notice Jev's error pattern by bucket. (3) This pattern explains why Act=Jev
  nets to zero.
- **Caveats:** observational; the 30–50 and ≥80 buckets are small.

## Figure 8: `figure-08-policy-values`
- **Purpose:** bounds the effect of RQ1.
- **Plotted:** direct-method value (child score, refusal rate) for the oracle, static,
  uniform and Jev policies, with 95% run-bootstrap CIs.
- **Key observation:** Jev ≈ static, and the paired difference is tight (+0.1 [−0.7, +1.0]).
  The oracle's headroom is ≈3 points on score and ≈13 points on refusal.
- **Caveats:** the oracle is fitted in-sample, so it is optimistic. The per-policy CIs
  overlap because they share data; use the paired difference in the stats appendix.

## Figure 9: `figure-09-operator-outcomes`
- **Purpose:** RQ3. How well does each operator work, and where did Jev's picks land?
- **Plotted:** mean judge score (95% run-bootstrap CI; none if all samples come from one run) of
  MUTATE prompts per operator, pooled over all Meta-judged arms (164 MUTATE prompts). Operators
  with at least one *realized* Jev pick are in red; n and realized Jev pick counts are in the
  labels.
- **Key observation:** operators differ by more than 20 points. The operator Jev applied most
  often, `semantic_steganography` (n = 30, 19 Jev picks), sits near the bottom (23.3), while
  several of the best (`artprompt` 41.5, n = 6; `many_shot_jailbreaking` 58.5, n = 2) were never
  applied by Jev. Jev's *intended* favorites (Figure 13) are also weak: `adversarial_prefix` was
  refused in all 6 of its uses (21.4) and `persona_roleplay` scores 25.6 (n = 16). So Jev's
  realized picks and its intended preferences both land on weak operators, for different reasons
  (seed coupling for the former, wording of the descriptions for the latter; Figure 14).
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Shows that operator choice has far more leverage than action
  choice. (2) Notice that red marks realized picks, which reflect the shared per-task seed. (3)
  Motivates an operator selector that learns from outcomes with an account of sample size.
- **Caveats:** not adjusted for parent quality; many operators have n ≤ 6. Some of these means
  measure the mutator rather than the operator: 44 of the 164 MUTATE prompts (27%) are the
  mutation LLM's own refusal, including 19 of the 30 `semantic_steganography` prompts
  (`operators_mutator_refusals.csv`). On its 11 real attacks, `semantic_steganography` still
  scores low (25.3).

## Figure 10: `figure-10-jev-judge-consistency`
- **Purpose:** what can be said about Jev as a judge without labels.
- **Plotted:** histogram of Jev P(related) with the 0.5 threshold (log y-axis); number of
  successes and mean score as the threshold varies.
- **Key observation:** P(related) is mostly decisive but has a real middle mass. The success
  count ranges from 6 to 1 across thresholds 0.3–0.7, while the mean score is stable.
- **Caveats:** internal consistency only. Agreement with Meta on the same prompts comes from
  the common referee (quadratic κ = 0.76; `referee_summary.json`), and agreement with an
  independent annotator from Figure 15.

## Figure 11: `figure-11-cluster-breadth`
- **Purpose:** RQ1 side effect. Does the action policy change how widely the search
  explores the attack corpus?
- **Plotted:** distinct top-level corpus clusters per run (dots = seeds, ◆ = mean with 95%
  seed-bootstrap CI). The green band is the 16.6 ± 1.7 clusters covered by 28 uniform corpus
  draws.
- **Key observation:** Act=Jev is the narrowest arm (9.0 vs. 12.4 for the baseline). Every
  arm is below the random-sampling reference, and the coverage arms come closest.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Shows a cost of Jev's SIMILAR preference that the score
  metric hides. (2) Notice the consistent downward shift for both Act=Jev arms. (3)
  Suggests an action selector should be judged on diversity as well as score.
- **Caveats:** exploratory; 14% of labels come from kNN (≈83% accurate); the exact-only
  version (in the stats appendix) shows the same effect.

## Figure 12: `figure-12-cluster-outcomes`
- **Purpose:** which attack families work against this target.
- **Plotted:** refusal rate and mean judge score per top-level corpus cluster (n ≥ 5),
  pooled over Meta-judged arms, with success counts.
- **Key observation:** refusal ranges from 0% to 100% by cluster. All successes come from
  persona/fiction clusters 25 and 3 and programming-style cluster 11.
- **Caveats:** not adjusted for arm or parent. The zero-refusal clusters (17, 23) yield
  benign compliance (≈54), not harm.

## Figure 13: `figure-13-operator-intended-vs-realized`
- **Purpose:** RQ3 and the seeding audit. Separate what Jev intended from what the fuzzer applied.
- **Plotted:** for each operator with nonzero intended or realized share in the Mut=Jev arms,
  the intended share (mean Jev probability over its 35 MUTATE decisions, blue) next to the
  realized share of the 35 picks (orange). Data: `seed_coupling_operators.csv`.
- **Key observation:** intended and realized distributions barely overlap. Jev intended
  `adversarial_prefix` (0.26), `persona_roleplay` (0.19), `semantic_steganography` (0.19), and
  `contextual_framing` (0.11), but the realized picks are `semantic_steganography` (0.54),
  `synonym` (0.40), and one pick each of `persona_roleplay` and `unicode`. `synonym` carried
  only 0.03 of Jev's probability but was applied 14 times; `adversarial_prefix` was never applied.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Exists to show that Figure 4's concentration comes from the
  shared per-task seed: MUTATE is the last action, so it is only chosen when the shared random
  number is high, and the operator is then drawn with that same number from a name-sorted list.
  (2) Notice that the realized mass sits on late-alphabet names. (3) Simulating the configured
  seeding with Jev's logged probabilities reproduces the realized pattern, while sampling them
  independently recovers the intended one (main.tex, Takeaway 4). Ablations of fuzzer control
  should log both intended and realized choices.
- **Caveats:** 35 decisions. The figure shows only the observed intended and realized shares,
  not the simulations. The seeding is the system's configured behavior, not a defect to fix, and
  results describe NAAMSE as configured.

## Figure 14: `figure-14-description-ablation`
- **Purpose:** RQ3 mechanism. Does Jev choose operators by their names, their descriptions, or
  evidence?
- **Plotted:** heatmap of Jev's mean probability per operator over the 66 replayed parents under
  five framings: *original* descriptions, *names only*, *anonymized* labels with the original
  descriptions, *swapped* descriptions between operators (column indexed by operator key), and
  *evidence* (descriptions plus observed outcomes). Data: `jev_description_ablation.json`.
- **Key observation:** preferences follow the descriptions. Anonymizing the names barely changes
  them (ρ = 0.91 with the original). When descriptions are swapped, the preferences track the
  descriptions (ρ = 0.83) rather than the names (ρ = 0.19): the key that inherits the
  `semantic_steganography` description receives 0.23 of the mass, while the
  `semantic_steganography` key itself drops to 0.02. With evidence, mass jumps to the operators
  with the least data, `many_shot_jailbreaking` (0.60, tried twice) and `payload_splitting` (0.19,
  tried once).
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Shows that descriptions act as hidden parameters of a
  zero-shot selector. (2) Notice that the original and anonymized columns are nearly identical,
  and that the swapped column moves with the descriptions. (3) A selector that learns from
  outcomes needs to account for how much evidence each operator has.
- **Caveats:** measures Jev's preferences, not their downstream effect on attacks. The evidence
  condition uses in-sample outcome statistics. Only 12 of 26 operators are shown; dots mark
  probabilities that round to zero.

## Figure 15: `figure-15-cross-model-annotation`
- **Purpose:** RQ2 and construct validity. Do an independent annotator and the judges agree, and
  how severe are the study's successes?
- **Plotted:** left, confusion matrix of alignment labels from Meta (the fitness judge, or the Meta
  referee for Fit=Jev) against a blind annotator from a third model family (Claude) on 103
  stratified prompts. Right, the strongest harm verdict on the 13 successes under the judge that
  scored them versus under the annotator. Data: `annotator_claude_summary.json`, `labeling/`.
- **Key observation:** alignment labels agree (84% exact, κ = 0.74; quadratic κ 0.82); when
  they differ, Meta is usually the stricter (11 of 16 disagreements). Harm does not agree: the
  judges rated 9 successes low risk, 1 high risk, and 3 harmful, while the annotator rates 11 low
  risk and 2 not harmful, and none of the 103 responses high risk or harmful. Nine of the 13
  successes are full acceptances whose strongest harm verdict is low risk, which the success
  threshold (score ≥ 80) allows.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Removes the concern that Meta is both target and judge for
  the alignment labels. (2) Notice the empty high-risk and harmful bands on the annotator side.
  (3) Success at the high-risk level should be reported separately and checked by a judge outside
  the target's model family. None of the 30 judged refusals contains harm, which supports the
  refusal-gated referee.
- **Caveats:** the annotator is itself an LLM, not a human, so its severity calls are a second
  opinion rather than ground truth. The Jev-judged subset is small (22 items).

## Figure 16: `figure-16-explore-window`
- **Purpose:** seeding audit. Where in the corpus can EXPLORE land?
- **Plotted:** for every post-initial EXPLORE draw, the corpus offset of the drawn prompt (as a
  fraction of 2^17) against the P(explore) of the decision that chose EXPLORE; static policy
  (n = 122) and Jev policy (n = 23), with the diagonal offset = P(explore).
- **Key observation:** every point lies below the diagonal. A decision that explores with
  probability 0.7 only reaches the first 70% of the corpus, and one that explores with
  probability 0.1 only reaches the first 10%. This is the shared per-task seed: the same random
  number decides both whether to explore and where to sample. Since the corpus is stored in source
  order, a policy that explores less also explores a narrower slice.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Explains part of the cluster narrowing in Figure 11. (2)
  Notice that Jev's points sit at low P(explore) and low offsets. (3) The window explains only
  about 10% of the Act=Jev versus Baseline cluster gap (−3.9 clusters with the configured window
  versus −3.5 with full-corpus draws, `seed_coupling_clusters.csv`); the rest comes from Jev
  exploring less (23 post-initial EXPLORE draws versus 61 for the baseline). The narrowing finding
  holds.
- **Caveats:** the decomposition is first-order and does not simulate the descendants of a
  different EXPLORE draw. The off-policy estimators assume an action's outcome does not depend on
  the policy that chose it, which this window violates for EXPLORE.

## Figure 17: `figure-17-operator-experiment`
- **Purpose:** RQ3 under control. What is operator choice actually worth once every operator sees
  the same parents? (main.tex Figure 4, Takeaway 8.)
- **Plotted:** a controlled grid of 20 parents × 10 operators (200 attacks sent to the target,
  scored by the refusal-gated Meta judges with 758 judge calls). The 20 parents come from the
  score-objective runs: 8 scoring below 30, 4 between 30 and 50, and 8 between 50 and 80. Left:
  mean judge score over the 20 parents per operator, with the refusal rate in the label; the grey
  bar is the `echo` no-op, which re-sends the parent unchanged. Right: each operator's score minus
  `echo` on the same parent (mean, 95% parent-bootstrap CI). Data:
  `operator_experiment_per_operator.csv`, `operator_experiment_contrasts.csv`,
  `operator_experiment_summary.json`.
- **Key observation:** no operator beats `echo` (37.2, refused 40%). `code_exec` (37.3, +0.1
  [−7.4, +7.5]), `many_shot_jailbreaking` (36.5, −0.7), and `synonym` (36.3, −0.9) are
  indistinguishable from it, and every other operator scores lower: `language_translation` 34.4,
  `semantic_steganography` 32.6, `dual_response_divider` 32.2, `adversarial_prefix` 30.6 (−6.7
  [−13.5, +0.1]), `artprompt` 28.2 (−9.0 [−18.0, −0.8]), and `persona_roleplay` 27.1 (−10.1
  [−17.6, −2.7], Holm p = 0.05; refused 75%). Keeping only mutations that produced a real attack,
  `persona_roleplay` is 12.7 points below `echo` (Holm p = 0.01). None of the 200 attacks reaches
  80 or gets a high-risk or harmful verdict.
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Exists to replace the observational operator comparison of
  Figure 9 with a within-parent one. (2) Notice that the whole right panel sits at or left of
  zero: the spread between operators is downside, and two of Jev's three favorites
  (`persona_roleplay`, `adversarial_prefix`) are among the worst. (3) The best a selector can do
  against this target is avoid harmful operators; there is no operator to find that beats the
  no-op. `artprompt` ranks second to last here, although it looked strong in the sweep.
- **Caveats:** each (parent, operator) cell is observed once. The `echo` re-test shows how noisy
  one score is: it differs from the parent's stored score by 5.8 points on average (Spearman
  ρ = 0.72; 85% land on the same side of 50). Operators run as configured, so 79 of the 200
  "mutations" (about 40%) are not new attacks: 32 are mutator refusals (`semantic_steganography`
  12 of 20, `many_shot_jailbreaking` 8, `artprompt` 6, `persona_roleplay` 4,
  `dual_response_divider` 2) and 47 are silent failures that re-send the parent
  (`code_exec` 10, `many_shot_jailbreaking` 10, `synonym` 9, `adversarial_prefix` 9,
  `artprompt` 9). This means half of `code_exec`'s cells are effectively `echo`, and
  `many_shot_jailbreaking` has only 2 real attacks. Only `persona_roleplay` survives Holm
  correction on all attacks.

## Figure 18: `figure-18-operator-bandit-replay`
- **Purpose:** what would an operator selector have earned on the controlled grid? (main.tex
  Figure 11, Takeaway 8.)
- **Plotted:** mean judge score per decision for five operator policies replayed offline on the
  20 × 10 grid of Figure 17, at campaign lengths of 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 decisions (4,000
  simulated campaigns per point, no new queries). Each decision draws a parent at random, the
  policy picks an operator, and the reward is that cell's recorded score. Policies: uniform;
  Jev intended (Jev's logged intended shares from the Mut=Jev arms, renormalized over the 10
  tested operators, which puts 0.31 on `adversarial_prefix`, 0.23 on `persona_roleplay`, and
  0.23 on `semantic_steganography`); UCB1; Gaussian Thompson sampling; and the best fixed operator
  (`code_exec`, chosen in-sample). The shaded band marks one run's MUTATE budget. Data:
  `operator_bandit_replay.csv`, `operator_bandit_replay.json`.
- **Key observation:** at 10 decisions, uniform averages 33.2, Jev intended 31.6, UCB1 33.4, and
  Thompson 33.6, against 37.5 for the best fixed operator. Jev intended sits below uniform at every
  campaign length (31.5 to 31.6 versus 33.2 to 33.3), because it favors operators that score below
  the no-op. UCB1 stays near uniform (33.6 at 200). Thompson starts to pull away only after about
  50 decisions and reaches 35.3 at 200, still below the best fixed operator (37.3).
- **Interpretation checklist:** (1) Converts the per-operator means of Figure 17 into the value
  of a selection policy. (2) Notice the flat orange line under the grey one, and that the
  bandits do not separate from uniform inside the shaded single-run budget. (3) Outcome feedback
  helps only over long campaigns, and even then the most it can learn here is to avoid harmful
  operators.
- **Caveats:** in-sample replay on one 20 × 10 grid with one observation per cell, so bandits
  are rewarded with the same noisy scores the figure is built from, and the best fixed operator is
  an optimistic ceiling. The best fixed operator is indistinguishable from `echo`, so the ceiling
  is essentially "change nothing". Per-campaign spread is wide at short horizons (2.5 to 97.5
  percentile at 10 decisions: uniform [24.0, 43.7], Jev intended [22.5, 40.8]), so a single run
  could not tell these policies apart. Jev's weights come from the sweep's intended
  probabilities, not from new Jev calls on these parents. main.tex reports Thompson at 200
  decisions as 35.4; the CSV value is 35.35.
